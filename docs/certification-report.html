Plateau State Grassroots Revenue Platform

An independent assessment of whether this application can safely be trusted with real government revenue and real taxpayer information — conducted by driving the running system against a live PostgreSQL database and attacking its stated guarantees from outside.

Not production ready

The application logic is sound. The system around it does not yet exist.

Every financial invariant this platform claims survived direct attack, including attacks that bypass the application entirely and write to the database as a compromised service account would. That work is genuinely strong. But a platform is not only its code: there is no deployment mechanism, no backup, no restore, no monitoring, and not one of the five external integrations has ever exchanged a packet with the real provider it will depend on. Certification fails on operational readiness, not on correctness.

Revision 10. Two hundred and twelve commits landed after revision 9, four of them changing rules this report had certified: a receipt now requires settlement rather than gateway confirmation, device binding acquired a relaxation for demonstrations, an agent’s commission account became changeable, and the last open code finding — the per-instance rate limiter, D-3 — was fixed. Adversarial reviews were commissioned to attack those changes, none written by the hand that made them. Fifteen defects came out of those reviews, two of them critical; two more came out of the verification runs that followed. Every one is fixed and pinned by a test (D-47 to D-63).

Four of those sixteen are the same mistake in four places, and it is worth naming because it is what this revision is really about. Each checked that a link existed rather than what the link pointed at: the receipt trigger asked whether a payment had a settlement_id and not whether that settlement had received anything; the reconciliation sweep asked the same question and called a batch the bank paid short “matched”; and two officer queues aged money from the last time a sweep looked rather than from when the money came in. Migration 040 had raised the bar to “the State has the money” and the code beneath it was still testing for “paperwork exists”. Two more of the sixteen were tests that protected a defect rather than catching it — one asserted that an officer’s private note about a taxpayer was returned to anonymous callers, which is how that leak survived every previous pass.

Three rules the service enforced correctly are now held in the database as well (migration 053), which is this report’s own standard for what counts as an invariant; the route count was retaken at 189 and the two routes nothing reached were read and covered; and D-3 is closed — not because the implementer says so, but because a review that set out to break the shared rate limiter found a real bypass in it (D-47) and, once that was fixed, could not break it again. The verdict does not move: it was already FAIL on B-4, and B-4 is not a code finding.

Revision 9. A form-by-form pass over both applications, asking of every field what happens when somebody types the wrong thing into it. Thirteen defects and three features the platform had been missing since it was written, of which four matter.

It also closed the one gap this pass recorded rather than fixed: an agent’s commission bank account can now be changed, through a flow the schema had been waiting for since the beginning — step-up, the bank’s own confirmation, a second officer, a payout-in-flight block, and a message to the agent the moment a change is requested.

The sharpest defect is not a missing control but an invisible one. The API refuses a commission payout approved by the officer who requested it — separation of duties, correctly implemented and carefully worded. The portal ran that call outside any error handling, so the refusal became an uncaught promise and the screen did not change. The officer pressed Approve, typed a reason, and nothing happened. Three other money-releasing actions behaved the same way. A control nobody is told about is a control that gets worked around.

The first is a disclosure. /citizen-status is the platform’s one unauthenticated read of a named person’s record, and it answered a phone number with that person’s TIN, their numeric compliance score, the names of the taxes they owe and the benefit programmes they qualify for. A phone number is not a secret. The TIN is the sharper half: the caller supplied the weaker identifier and was handed the stronger one, and this platform’s own duplicate detection treats a matching TIN as identity-grade where a shared phone scores only 85 of the 100 needed to block. Under the incentive design a compliance score decides access to fertiliser, health insurance and farm inputs, which makes it consequential enough to be nobody else’s business. The endpoint now answers only whether anything is owed, whether a TIN has been issued, and where to go to be identified properly. The full record remains available through the agent and officer channels, which establish who they are speaking to first.

The second is a date of birth nobody can hold. dateOfBirth was declared as z.string().date() over a bare DATE column, so the string was checked for shape and nothing else at any of the three layers; 2099 passed the wizard, the API and the database. Date of birth is one of the fields duplicate detection weighs and is printed on the taxpayer’s identity card, so a row nobody can be born into corrupts both. The rule now lives in the shared package, and migration 021 adds the matching constraint.

The remaining three are smaller but of a kind: a rate-change form that turned an empty amount into a rate of ₦0 and read “5abc” as five per cent; a taxpayer search that answered a search matching nobody by not changing at all; and a yes/no toggle whose selection was visible only to people who could see it. In each case the form invented a value or an outcome the person never chose.

Revision 8. Eleven further defects, found by reading the routes no test exercised and by driving each surface in a real browser rather than reasoning about it. Three are serious: the audit log took the actor’s role from a field in the request body, so an agent could file their own change as a revenue officer’s; a spent referee link could overturn a clearance an officer had already granted; and commission frozen while a fraud flag was investigated was never released when the flag was dismissed, so an agent who was investigated and cleared could not be paid by any route short of a manual database write.

Two of the eleven were introduced by this revision’s own work and are recorded as such (D-25, D-32). An audit that only reports faults it inherited is not measuring itself.

Revision 3. Five of the original six blockers are now closed and re-verified at commit 4ab5925: the demonstration administrator, the localhost public URLs, deployment, backup and recovery, and observability. The restoration test the brief requires was performed rather than planned — a database holding a real verified payment was dumped, dropped entirely, and restored, and the receipt came back identical with the controls still firing.

One blocker remains, and it cannot be closed from the repository. None of the five external integrations has ever exchanged a packet with the provider it will depend on. That needs PSIRS credentials and vendor sandboxes. A verification harness now exists to close it; someone has to run it.

The repository does not claim otherwise. README.md §Status and docs/SECURITY.md §Known gaps both state plainly that integration and operations work remains. This assessment confirms that self-description is accurate, quantifies what is outstanding, and adds five defects the project had not recorded.

This platform is built on an unusual and correct architectural decision: its financial guarantees live in the database, not in application code. A receipt cannot be inserted for an unverified payment because a PostgreSQL trigger refuses it. Transactions, payments, receipts, commissions and audit rows cannot be deleted or silently amended because triggers refuse that too. I attacked those rules directly with SQL, as a compromised service account or a careless DBA would, and every one of them held.

The money path is equally disciplined. No route anywhere accepts a payment status from its caller; confirmPayment takes no outcome argument and always re-asks the gateway. Ten replayed webhook deliveries produced one receipt and one commission. Ten concurrent payment initiations produced one payment row. Commission computed to the kobo at ₦1,000, ₦10,000, ₦100,000 and ₦1,000,000, and — stricter than the brief requires — will not be released until the transaction is SETTLED, meaning government has actually been paid, not merely that a gateway said so.

What fails is everything between that code and a running government service. There is no Dockerfile, no infrastructure definition, no deployment pipeline, no backup configuration and no monitoring. A restoration test — which the brief requires rather than a claim that backups exist — could not be performed because there is nothing to restore from. And the five adapters that connect this platform to Remita, the PSIRS TIN service, a KYC vendor, the vehicle registry and bank verification have only ever spoken to development mocks. The boot guard correctly refuses to start in production while any adapter is still mock, but no guard can detect a wrong field mapping against a real provider.

Separately, I found five defects the project had not recorded, two of which would have caused visible harm on day one: every receipt would have carried a QR code pointing at localhost, and the documented seeding command invited an operator to create a government administrator account with a published password in production. Both have since been fixed and pinned by regression tests.

Revision 3. Deployment, backup and recovery, and observability have since been built and verified — including the restoration test the brief requires, performed against a real database holding a real payment. Five of the six blockers are closed. The one that remains is the one no code change can close: five integrations that have never been asked a real question.

Blockers

Each of these independently prevents certification. None is hidden in the recommendations below. Five have since been resolved and are retained, marked Fixed, so the record of what was wrong survives the fix. B-4 remains open.

B-1

No deployment mechanism of any kind existed

Fixed

The repository contained no Dockerfile, no infrastructure as code, no orchestration manifest and no deployment pipeline. CI built and tested; nothing deployed, so there was no rollback strategy because there was nothing to roll back.

Fixed in
4ab5925. A multi-stage Dockerfile shipping no compiler, tests or dev dependencies and running as node; a docker-compose.yml for acceptance environments; and .github/workflows/deploy.yml, whose ordering is the substance — test against a real database, tag by commit so a rollback names an exact artefact, back up before migrating, migrate once as its own job, roll out, revert automatically on failed health checks.
Also fixed
Two defects that made multi-instance deployment unsafe: background workers now take a PostgreSQL advisory lock instead of a module-level boolean (D-1), and migrations take one too so simultaneous boots cannot race (D-2).
Verified
The image could not be built here — Docker Hub's blob CDN is blocked by this environment's network policy — so the substance was verified instead: the exact build commands were run, and the resulting artefact was booted in full production configuration. It migrates, listens, logs JSON, serves both health endpoints, refuses /metrics without its token, and drains cleanly on SIGTERM. The image build itself remains unverified.

B-2

No backup, no recovery procedure, and no restoration test

Fixed

Nothing in the repository configures database backups, retention, or point-in-time recovery, and no disaster recovery procedure is documented. docs/PRD-TRACEABILITY.md records RPO ≤15 min and RTO ≤2 h as targets to be agreed, not as implemented capabilities. The brief explicitly requires an actual restoration test rather than confirmation that backups exist; that test cannot be attempted.

Consequence
A storage failure would have lost every receipt, every transaction and the entire audit trail, permanently and with no stated recovery path.
Fixed in
4ab5925. backup.sh dumps, then verifies the archive is readable and contains the financial tables, then records a checksum — pg_dump exits 0 on archives pg_restore cannot read. restore.sh checks that checksum, restores with --exit-on-error, and then proves the restored database still refuses a receipt with no verified payment. docs/DISASTER-RECOVERY.md carries the runbook, the WAL configuration for a 15-minute RPO, and the reconciliation step that makes an RPO gap survivable.
Restoration test
Performed, not planned. A database holding a real verified payment was dumped, then DROP DATABASE, then restored into an empty one. Receipt PSIRS/2026/000001 came back with identical amount and verification code, all 14 audit rows intact, 16 integrity triggers present — and a forged receipt was still refused afterwards: "Receipt amount (999999 kobo) does not match verified payment amount (20000 kobo)". That last check is the difference between the rows being back and the platform being back.
Regression
backup-restore.test.ts runs the same cycle on every CI run, including refusing a deliberately corrupted archive.
Still outstanding
Scheduling, an offsite bucket, and WAL archiving on the production instance — all of which need infrastructure that does not exist yet.

B-3

No production observability — failures would have been invisible

Fixed

The platform has a /health endpoint that checks database connectivity, and 62 console.* calls. There is no error tracking, no metrics endpoint, no structured logging, no uptime monitoring, no alerting, and no background-job monitoring. The reconciliation sweep logs an ABORTED run to standard output and nothing else; a reconciliation that stopped running would look exactly like one finding no exceptions.

Consequence
Failed webhooks, stalled workers and unreturned refunds would have accumulated silently.
Fixed in
4ab5925. A structured logger emitting one JSON object per line in production, carrying the correlation id the response already returns, with every field passed through a redactor rather than trusting each call site to remember PRD §62. A /metrics endpoint exporting confirmation counts, webhook outcomes, per-worker liveness and — the point — the queue depths of what the platform still owes, read from the database at scrape time. Error reporting as an adapter in the same shape as SMS and KYC, with no vendor SDK, because an SDK here runs inside the failure path it exists to report.
Enforced
Production now refuses to boot with ERROR_REPORTING still mock or METRICS_TOKEN unset, for the same reason it already refused a mock payment gateway.
Verified
17 tests covering redaction (including nested contexts and hostile input), exposition-format validity, a real payment counted end to end, every queue gauge, both health endpoints, and reporter failure isolation. Confirmed live against the built artefact: /metrics returned 401 without a token and real gauges with one.

B-4

Every external integration is unproven against its real provider

Blocker

Payment (Remita), TIN, KYC, vehicle registry and bank verification all run against development mocks. Each adapter reads its provider's response through configurable field paths and maps the provider's status vocabulary onto the platform's — and none of those mappings has been confirmed against a sandbox. The design fails closed where it can (an unrecognised KYC status becomes UNDER_REVIEW; an unmapped Remita code stays PENDING), which is the right instinct, but a mapping that is wrong rather than unrecognised is not protected by that.

Specifically unconfirmed
Remita service type ID and status code set; the PSIRS TIN format and field names; the contracted KYC vendor's status vocabulary; the vehicle registry's response shape; the bank verification endpoint.
Why it stays open
This cannot be closed from the repository. It needs PSIRS credentials and live vendor sandboxes. No code change substitutes for asking the real service a real question.
Mitigation in place
config.ts refuses to boot in production while any adapter is mock — verified in a child process during this audit. Every adapter also fails closed on an unrecognised answer: an unmapped KYC status becomes UNDER_REVIEW, an unmapped Remita code stays PENDING. Correct, and no protection against a mapping that is wrong rather than unrecognised.
Added in 4ab5925
npm run verify:integrations asks each configured provider a real question and reports what the platform made of the answer, exiting non-zero if any answer could not be acted on — usable as a go-live gate. It deliberately fails on UNDER_REVIEW and PENDING, because those are valid results that are indistinguishable from a bad mapping. docs/INTEGRATION-VERIFICATION.md lists every field path and status word that must be confirmed, per provider, with the failure each wrong one produces.
Kept verified, not merely verified once
A sign-off decays. A mapping confirmed in March is not confirmed in September — a vendor adds a status code, an environment variable is edited, a sandbox is reprovisioned — and none of those announce themselves. They surface as payments stuck on PENDING and agents who are never cleared, which is the failure this blocker exists to prevent. .github/workflows/integration-verification.yml now runs the harness daily against whatever the repository secrets point at, so the answer is never more than a day old, and keeps the run output as evidence for 180 days.

Until those secrets exist the job reports skipped, naming what is missing. Deliberately not failed — a red cross every morning for a condition nobody can fix that morning teaches people to ignore red crosses. Deliberately not passed — a green tick would assert these integrations were verified. That "not checked" must never be able to look like "checked" is the whole substance of this blocker, so the job is built to preserve the distinction rather than paper over it.
Closes when
The harness exits 0 against real sandboxes, one live payment has been collected and its receipt verified publicly, and one agent has been cleared through the real KYC provider. Adding the credentials is what closes it; the scheduled job is what keeps it closed.

The seed command offered to create a production administrator with a published password

Fixed

seedDemoAgent() refuses to run when NODE_ENV=production. seedDemoUsers() has no such guard. Running npm run seed -- --demo against a production database creates five ACTIVE government accounts — including role admin at +2348000000001 — all with the password Password123.

This is not a hypothetical misuse. npm run seed is the only way to load the reference catalogue (LGAs, wards, revenue items) that production genuinely needs, so an operator setting up production will run it — and on completion it prints: "Re-run with --demo to add demonstration government users." The tool actively recommends the dangerous action, in production, with no guard.

Consequence
Full administrative compromise of the platform by anyone who reads the repository.

B-6

Public URLs defaulted to localhost, and the production boot guard did not check them

Fixed

I booted config.ts with a fully production-shaped environment — real gateway, real TIN service, real KYC provider, real registry, real bank verification, S3 storage, 40-character secrets — and it started cleanly with:

callbackUrl : http://localhost:5173/payment/return verificationBaseUrl : http://localhost:5174/verify corsOrigins : ["http://localhost:5173","http://localhost:5174"]

The same environment at 173be39:

Error: Refusing to start in production with development integrations: VERIFICATION_BASE_URL still points at a local address ("http://localhost:5174/verify"); PAYMENT_CALLBACK_URL still points at a local address ("http://localhost:5173/payment/return"); CORS_ORIGINS entry "http://localhost:5173" still points at a local address (...); CORS_ORIGINS entry "http://localhost:5174" still points at a local address (...)

VERIFICATION_BASE_URL is QR-encoded and printed onto every receipt PDF and every vehicle renewal certificate, and it forms every referee invitation link. Unset, that means: every citizen who scans their receipt to check it is genuine reaches nothing; every referee invitation is unfollowable, so no agent can ever complete clearance and the entire onboarding pipeline stops. Because receipts are immutable by design, receipts issued before anyone notices carry the broken code permanently.

PAYMENT_CALLBACK_URL unset sends the taxpayer's browser to a dead page at the moment immediately after they part with money. The payment itself still verifies by webhook, so no revenue is lost — but the citizen has no way to know that.

Was
The production guard checked eleven settings and not these three.
Fixed in
173be39. The guard now refuses a local address, plain HTTP, a malformed URL or an empty origin list for all three settings, and .env.example explains why each matters.
Verified
The exact environment that booted clean above is now refused, naming all four offending values. Plain HTTP on a real domain, 127.0.0.1 and a malformed URL are each refused; correct HTTPS addresses boot. Three regression tests pin this.

Method and evidence

I did not accept the existing test suite as evidence. I started PostgreSQL 16, applied all 15 migrations, ran the platform's own 457 tests to establish a baseline (all passed), and then wrote 39 further tests whose purpose was to break the system's stated guarantees rather than demonstrate them.

Later revisions kept the same rule and added two methods to it. Revision 7 measured the platform under load instead of asserting it would hold. Revision 8 enumerated every mounted route, read the ones no test touched, and drove each user-facing surface in a real browser — which is where nine of its eleven defects came from, and why several of them had survived every previous pass: they were all invisible to anyone reading the code with the assumption that a written route is a reached one.

Revision 10 re-examined the rules that had changed underneath it. Two hundred and twelve commits landed after revision 9, and among them were changes to the rules themselves: a receipt now requires settlement rather than gateway confirmation, device binding acquired a relaxation for demonstrations, an agent’s commission account became changeable, and the last open code finding — the per-instance rate limiter, D-3 — was fixed in the same working session that produced this revision, by the same hand that is writing it.

An auditor who marks their own fix as passing has certified nothing. So the verification of D-3, and of every money-rule and access-control change since revision 9, was delegated to adversarial reviews, each on its own database, each briefed to break the control rather than confirm it, each required to bypass the service layer with raw SQL wherever the database is claimed to enforce something, and each required to commit its attacks as tests that fail if the control gives. The first attempt at all four died on an API rate limit before reporting anything; they were re-run, and the three that completed are the substance of this revision. Their suites are committed as certification-rev10-money.test.ts (28 assertions), certification-rev10-access.test.ts (29) and certification-rev10-rate-limit.test.ts (11).

They left twenty-four assertions failing. Seventeen defects came out of this revision, listed as D-47 to D-63 and each now fixed; the rest were the reviews’ own harnesses misreading the platform’s contract, and are recorded as such rather than quietly dropped. Two of the sixteen are critical: a single finance officer could mint government receipts for money no bank ever sent (D-48), and a gateway answering REVERSED — money given back — produced a verified payment, an acknowledgement to the taxpayer and commission accruing to the agent (D-50).

Triage was not always right first time. Three failures were reported to the reader of this work as the auditor’s harness being wrong, without the assertion messages having been read; two of the three were real defects, and one was catching a bug in the fix that had been written an hour earlier. That correction is recorded in D-51, because a report whose method section insists on evidence should say when its own author asserted something without it.

What this revision claims beyond the reviews, and can show: the dependency advisory in D-46, traced to its two call sites in node_modules and closed; the inventory counts, each read from the codebase rather than carried forward; the Hausa coverage in D-45, counted in the dictionary and pinned by eight consistency tests; the portal’s test position in D-5; and the route count, retaken at 189 declared and 189 exercised, with the counting tool committed this time so the figure can be re-derived rather than remembered.

One area changed since revision 9 and is still not re-verified here: the vehicle-renewal happy path against a real registry, which is D-4 and was already open. The four that revision 10 set out to audit — the settlement-gated receipt, the device-binding relaxation, the commission bank-account change, and route coverage — have each been attacked and are reported above.

Where a guarantee is claimed to be enforced in the database, the audit bypasses the application entirely and issues SQL directly — the position of a compromised service account or a DBA at a psql prompt. A rule that only holds when you go through the service layer is not an invariant, and this distinction is what separates a real control from a convention.

Several of my early failures were my own harness being wrong about the system's contract, and each one was a control working correctly: taxpayer registration refused to proceed without explicit consentGiven and declarationAccepted; payment initiation refused to proceed without an Idempotency-Key header; the gateway simulation endpoint refused an unauthenticated caller; consumption tax refused to apply to an individual taxpayer. I corrected the harness rather than the platform in every case.

07

Financial integrity

This is the strongest part of the system and it deserves to be described precisely, because the design choice behind it is the reason the certification failure is operational rather than architectural.

The rule "no verified payment, no government receipt" is a BEFORE INSERT trigger on receipts. It checks that the referenced payment exists, is VERIFIED, carries both a verified_at timestamp and a gateway reference, belongs to the same transaction, matches the amount exactly, and that the transaction is in a state that permits issuance. A CHECK constraint separately forbids a payment row from claiming VERIFIED without recording what verified it. The practical effect is that there is no code path — API, migration, maintenance script or human at a prompt — that produces a receipt for money that did not arrive.

Duplicate protection is layered the same way. A partial unique index permits only one payment per transaction in an in-flight state, so a double-tapped Pay button cannot create two obligations even if the application logic failed. UNIQUE (gateway, event_id) on the webhook table makes a redelivery detectable at insert time. An advisory lock serialises concurrent confirmations of one payment, and verification runs at SERIALIZABLE isolation because it reads and writes the payment, the invoice and the commission ledger together.

08

Security and access control

Authorisation is three separate gates that are deliberately not collapsed: authenticate() confirms a live, unrevoked session; requirePermission() checks the role's permission; requireActiveAgent() checks that the agent is operationally cleared, on a registered device, running a permitted app version. A signed-in agent and a cleared agent are different things, and the platform is careful about the difference.

Two details are worth singling out. The role is read from the database on every request rather than trusted from the token, so a suspension or role change applies to sessions issued before it — I confirmed this by suspending an agent mid-session and watching the very next request return AGENT_SUSPENDED. And step-up grants are consumed, not merely checked, so one OTP authorises exactly one high-risk action and a captured token cannot approve a second payout.

Segregation of duties

The admin role can configure the platform, manage users, approve agents and change the revenue catalogue — and cannot approve a payment reversal, a refund, or a commission payout. Those require finance_officer. The auditor role holds no mutating permission at all. This is real segregation, verified by both permission inspection and a live request: an administrator calling the reversal endpoint receives 403.

The brief asks about a "KYC Officer" and a "Super Administrator". Neither exists as a distinct role; KYC review sits with revenue_officer and admin, and admin is terminal with no tier above it. Both are defensible design decisions rather than defects, but the absence of a tier above admin means there is no one who can review an administrator's own configuration changes. Worth a decision from PSIRS rather than a silent inheritance.

There is no taxpayer role, and the code documents this as a deliberate product decision: a citizen never signs in, so there is no credential to phish and no session to hijack. They receive their receipt by SMS and verify it through a public page needing no account. This is a coherent security posture — and it is exactly the posture that defect D-6 breaks, since that public page is the citizen's only recourse.

Verified during this audit

  • A JWT signed with an attacker's own key is rejected (401).
  • An alg: none unsigned token is rejected (401).
  • Session revocation takes effect on the next request, not at token expiry.
  • Passwords are bcrypt-hashed; the plaintext appears nowhere.
  • A wrong password and an unknown phone number return byte-identical responses — no user enumeration.
  • Brute-force lockout is database-backed, so it survives a multi-instance deployment.
  • Webhook signatures are verified over the raw request bytes, not the re-serialised body.
  • An unsigned or forged webhook is recorded and refused, never acted on.
  • An anonymous document request is refused; another agent cannot read a KYC document.
  • Security headers include HSTS, nosniff, frame-options: DENY, a restrictive permissions policy and no-referrer.
  • Public receipt verification returns receipt number, revenue type, amount, date and LGA — and no phone, address, name or identity hash.

Defects

Blockers B-1 to B-6 are above. These are the remaining findings, none of which alone prevents certification but all of which should be resolved.

D-1

Background workers assumed a single instance

Fixed

All seven workers are setInterval timers inside the API process, and the reconciliation sweep's re-entrancy guard is a module-level boolean (sweepInFlight). Behind a load balancer with N instances, N reconciliation sweeps run concurrently every six hours, each issuing a status query to Remita for every payment reference in a 48-hour window. Confirmation is idempotent so no double receipts result, but the gateway load multiplies by instance count and may hit rate limits. There is no leader election.

Fixed in
4ab5925. withJobLock takes a session-scoped pg_try_advisory_lock and returns immediately rather than queueing — a sweep another instance is already running needs skipping, not repeating. Each run is also timed and counted, so a worker that stops running becomes an alert.
Verified
Three tests: a second caller is turned away rather than queued, a thrown job does not strand its lock, and a different job is not blocked.
D-2

Migrations ran on boot with no advisory lock

Fixed

server.ts calls runMigrations() before listening, and the runner takes no lock. Two instances starting together both read the applied set and both attempt the same file; one fails and calls process.exit(1). It fails safe rather than corrupting — DDL is transactional in PostgreSQL — but a rolling deploy will crash-loop replicas until the first finishes.

Fixed in
4ab5925. runMigrations holds pg_advisory_lock for the whole run, so a second instance waits and then finds the schema current. Separately, RUN_MIGRATIONS_ON_BOOT=false lets the pipeline own migrations entirely, which is what the deploy workflow does.
D-3

Rate limiting was per-instance

Fixed

The limiter keeps its buckets in a process-local Map. With N instances the effective limit is N times the configured maximum. It was documented in docs/SECURITY.md as a known gap awaiting a Redis store. The impact is bounded because account lockout — the control that actually stops credential stuffing — is database-backed.

Location
apps/api/src/middleware/security.ts:19
Changed in
7ece146. Counts move to PostgreSQL rather than Redis, because the platform’s stated shape is that the only state is PostgreSQL and a Redis would be the one component whose quiet loss weakens a security control without anything failing. The increment is a single INSERT … ON CONFLICT DO UPDATE … RETURNING rather than a read and a write, so concurrent hits on one key serialise on the row lock; a caller already over the limit is refused from a local cache until their window ends, so a flood costs one round trip rather than one per request; and the store fails open, because a bookkeeping table being unreachable must not stop collection statewide. Production refuses to boot on the per-process store.
Why it is now marked fixed
The change and this revision were written by the same hand in the same session, and an implementer’s own tests are the weakest evidence in this report: they encode what the implementer thought to check. So the finding stayed open until somebody who had not written the code tried to break it. That review ran — concurrency overshoot across instances, the local cache as a bypass, the fail-open path as an attacker-triggerable hole, raw-SQL manipulation of the bucket table, clock skew between the application and the database — and found a real bypass: an unbounded key, which the caller could make long enough to break the store’s own index and be let through (D-47). With that closed, all eleven of its attacks hold. Those eleven are the evidence for this finding, not the thirteen tests written alongside the fix.
What is left, and it is not code
The store is only shared if every instance points at the same database. Nothing in the application can check that; the deployment has to arrange it.

D-20

The audit chain caps the platform, and horizontal scaling cannot move it

Measured

This report previously recorded "Performance under load — Not tested" alongside the observation that the indexes look right. Those are different claims, and only one was evidenced. An index existing tells you a query can use it; not that the planner does, nor that a transaction is not serialising behind another.

audit chain append, c=1 p50 1.57ms p99 3.59ms about 637 appends/sec audit chain append, c=4 p50 6.67ms p99 12.93ms about 600 appends/sec audit chain append, c=16 p50 28.95ms p99 39.17ms about 553 appends/sec

Latency rises almost exactly in step with concurrency while total throughput stays flat and drifts slightly down. That is the signature of a resource behind one lock, and here the lock is deliberate: audit_logs is tamper-evident, each entry hashing its predecessor, so recordAudit serialises appends because two concurrent writers reading the same tail would fork a chain no verifier could replay.

The design is right. The finding is that nobody had measured what it costs. Every money movement writes audit entries, so this is the ceiling on recording revenue — on the order of 40 complete collections per second. Three things follow that are worth knowing before they matter:

  • Adding instances will not help. The lock is global.
  • It degrades as latency, not as errors. Collections queue rather than fail, so the symptom is agents saying the app is slow on market day — not an alert.
  • It is the first place to look if collection ever feels slow under load, because nothing else on the write path is serialised this way.
Comfortable for Plateau State
Roughly 150,000 collections an hour. No change is recommended: the remedies — batching appends, or sharding the chain per entity type — all weaken the single-chain guarantee, and none should be applied speculatively.
Read paths are healthy
TIN lookup, the path an agent hits for every taxpayer, stays under 5ms at p99 with 32 concurrent callers. Zero errors throughout. Every hot path uses an index, confirmed with EXPLAIN ANALYZE rather than inferred from pg_indexes — including the sector index that incentive targeting depends on.
Sustained load, now measured
Twelve one-minute windows at concurrency 4: 511,737 appends, zero errors. p50 drifted 5.23ms → 5.39ms (×1.03), throughput 721/s → 699/s (×0.97), heap +1.1MB, and the pool never had a waiter. The ceiling measured in a burst holds under sustained load; nothing leaks and nothing degrades.
The chain check was vacuous, and is not any more
The soak first reported “verifying the chain end to end… valid, 10000 entries replayed” after writing half a million. verifyAuditChain defaults to 10,000 rows from sequence 0, so it replayed the oldest ten thousand and touched nothing the run had produced — the same shape as the delete guard tested against empty tables. Both the soak and a standalone script now page to the highest sequence: 511,822 entries replayed, chain valid.
Still not measured
A full collection lifecycle at concurrency, the reconciliation sweep at volume, a soak over days rather than minutes, and any production topology. One machine, no network, warm cache: the absolute figures are a floor, not a forecast. The movement between concurrency levels is the part that travels.
Reproduce
npm run load-test; full results and limits in docs/LOAD-TEST-RESULTS.md.

D-47

The new rate limiter could be switched off by the caller it was limiting

Fixed

The shared rate-limit store fails open: if the database cannot be reached it allows the request and logs, on the reasoning that a bookkeeping table being unavailable must not stop collection statewide. That reasoning is sound. What it missed is that part of the key is written by the caller, so the caller could produce the error themselves.

On an ip-keyed surface the key is <prefix>:<req.clientIp>, and behind a load balancer (TRUST_PROXY=true — the topology §10 recommends) req.clientIp comes from X-Forwarded-For, a header the caller sends. key is the primary key of rate_limit_buckets and therefore carries a btree index, whose tuples cannot exceed 2704 bytes. A multi-kilobyte header made the insert fail, the store failed open, and the request was allowed — every request, without limit, on precisely the public surfaces the tight caps exist to protect: /citizen-status against TIN and phone enumeration, public receipt verification against code guessing, and POST /agents/apply against application farming.

DEFECT (fail-open bypass): 15/15 oversized-key requests were allowed comma-free oversized XFF -> req.ip length: 3000 matches what was sent: true
How it was found, and why that matters
By the adversarial review commissioned to attack the D-3 fix, on code written in the same session as this revision. The implementer’s own thirteen tests all passed and none of them contemplated a caller-supplied key. This is the finding that justifies the arrangement: an author does not attack their own reasoning, because the blind spot and the code come from the same place.
Two false negatives on the way to it
The attack nearly failed to land, twice, for reasons that are worth recording because both would have left the defect in place while appearing to clear it. The auditor built its oversized key from a repeated character — which TOAST compresses, so the row stored, the insert succeeded and the precondition failed loudly. Corrected to random printable bytes, the unit case caught the defect but the end-to-end case still passed: Express splits X-Forwarded-For on commas, and random printable filler contains one roughly every ninety bytes, so req.ip arrived as an 82-character fragment. Only a comma-free value reaches req.ip at its full length. A test can pass because the system is sound or because the attack was malformed, and the two are indistinguishable from the result alone.
Fix
A key longer than 512 bytes is folded to a readable 128-character head plus a SHA-256 of the whole, in the store, before the statement is built — so no key can be un-storable and the fail-open path is no longer reachable by anything a caller sends. Legitimate keys are far shorter and are returned untouched, so no existing bucket changes. Both attack cases now pass, with the preconditions corrected so they would fail if the fold were removed.
Carried forward
The limiter is fixed; req.clientIp is not. It still accepts three kilobytes of caller-supplied text and that value is written to audit_logs.ip_address, sessions and the payment source-IP columns by a dozen call sites. Nothing there fails open, so this is not a bypass — but an IP address field holding arbitrary attacker text is worth bounding at the source. (§11 item 4)

Essential public services were denied on tax arrears, past a safeguard that could not fire

Fixed

PRD §40 states that the platform must not deny essential public services merely because a person is not tax-compliant, unless the linkage is authorised by law or policy. createProgramme enforces it.

The Plateau State Health Insurance Scheme was seeded requiring no arrears and a compliance score of 40; the Scholarship and Bursary Scheme required 50. Both denied: a citizen behind on a levy was recorded ineligible for subsidised health cover. The safeguard was bypassed twice over, independently:

  1. By raw SQL. Both were inserted by a migration, so the service — and its guard — never ran.
  2. By naming. The guard matched the strings HEALTHCARE and EDUCATION; the programmes are typed HEALTH_INSURANCE and EDUCATION_BURSARY. It would not have fired even through the service. It was dead code protecting nothing.
Severity
The platform was making a determination with real consequences for a citizen's access to healthcare, against an explicit safeguard, with no record that anyone had decided to override it. Not a security defect — an accountability one, which in a government revenue system is not a lesser category.
Resolved by policy decision
Health insurance and the bursary are now ADDITIVE_BENEFIT: compliance sets the benefit tier and never withdraws the benefit. A citizen holding a TIN is in; paying on time moves them from base to full entitlement. Nothing is withdrawn from anybody, so §40 is satisfied without a statute to cite. The fertilizer programme and housing fund remain ELIGIBILITY_GATE — allocating a finite quantity of subsidised inputs, or extending credit, is choosing between claimants on something scarce rather than withdrawing a service.
Guard repaired
Matching is now by prefix, so a future HEALTH_SOMETHING cannot slip past on a name, and an essential-service programme must be either additive or carry a recorded legal basis. Both facts are written to the audit trail, because together they are the §40 decision: how the programme treats a non-compliant citizen, and on whose authority.
Thresholds kept, not zeroed
Under additive mode they no longer deny anyone; they separate the base entitlement from the full one, which is the reward the programmes were meant to offer. Zeroing them would have removed the incentive along with the penalty.

The only live part was recordRequest(), feeding a store nothing read.

verify-backup.sh hardcoded the database name psirs, while CI's database is psirs_test. The step failed with FATAL: database "psirs" does not exist the first time it ever executed — which was only after D-13 was fixed.

Fixed in

The three gauges genuinely missing from the registry that does serve /metrics — psirs_uptime_seconds, psirs_memory_bytes, psirs_db_pool_connections — are collected there, and both orphaned files deleted. psirs_http_requests_total and psirs_worker_runs_total were deliberately not carried over: both already exist with their own label schemes, and duplicate metric families make Prometheus reject the entire scrape rather than just those two series.

D-11

The auditor's read-only standing was not observable

Fixed

Raised from a user's own observation, not from the audit plan: that the portal looked the same whichever role signed in. Measuring it bore that out. Eleven of the fifteen menu items are common to revenue_officer, finance_officer, auditor and admin — and the auditor's menu was the administrator's menu plus one link, Reconciliation. The read-only oversight role saw more of the portal than the administrator did.

Most of that is correct and deliberate. These are all oversight roles and all meant to see the whole revenue picture; what separates them is what they may change, and that was already enforced — all 19 mutating controls across seven screens are wrapped in can(), and every navigation gate already matched the permission its screen's endpoint requires.

The defect was narrower and worth naming precisely. An auditor holds no mutating permission at all — the entire point of the role — and the portal conveyed that only by declining to render buttons, which is indistinguishable from a portal that forgot to. A government auditor's independence is part of the control environment, and an external reviewer should be able to observe it rather than infer it from an absence or take it from a permissions table.

Severity
Not a security defect. API authorisation was correct throughout and is verified independently in the RBAC matrix above. This is an assurance and usability defect: the control existed but could not be seen.
Fixed in

Deliberately not done

D-10

The TIN format guard was missing from the path that reaches the column it protects

Fixed

Found while testing whether a wrong integration mapping is detectable at all — the assumption the entire plan for closing B-4 rests on.

TIN_FORMAT_PATTERN exists for one stated reason: taxpayers.tin is UNIQUE on a row that cannot be deleted, so a malformed value written there is permanent and blocks the real number forever. register honoured the pattern. lookup did not — it returned whatever string sat at the configured field path, unchecked. And registerTaxpayer writes lookup.tin straight into that column on the existingTin path.

So the guard was absent from a path that reaches precisely the column it was written to protect. A TIN service answering a lookup with a differently-formatted value would have had it persisted, permanently.

Severity
Requires the TIN service to return a malformed value in the number field, so not directly attacker-controlled — but the damage is irreversible, which is the whole reason the guard exists.
Fixed in
38d8c73. Both paths now run the number through the same check, and an unreadable number reports UNAVAILABLE rather than FOUND, because neither "no number" nor "a number we cannot read" is the service saying the TIN does not exist.
Also added
mapping-detection.test.ts pins the property the verification harness depends on: a wrong mapping fails closed and lands on the outcome the harness reports as FAIL, not one it reports as OK. If a bad mapping were indistinguishable from a good one, running the harness against a sandbox would prove nothing.

10

Operational readiness

The gap between this codebase and a running government service is the whole of the certification failure, so it is worth stating plainly what is and is not there.

What exists. A CI pipeline that builds, typechecks, migrates and tests against a real PostgreSQL service container — a genuinely good choice, since the financial guarantees are triggers and a mocked database would verify nothing. Dependabot. A /health endpoint that checks database connectivity. Graceful shutdown that drains in-flight requests before closing the pool, with a hard timeout, so a payment confirmation is never cut off mid-transaction. A production boot guard that refuses eleven classes of misconfiguration.

What was missing, and now exists. A way to deploy the thing, with a rollback. A backup, a restore, and a rehearsal of the restore. Structured logs, metrics, an error-reporting path, and — the part that mattered most — an exported depth for every queue of what the platform still owes: the outstanding TINs, the unacknowledged renewals, the unreturned refunds, the unresolved reconciliation exceptions.

That last point was the shape of the whole problem. This platform is unusually careful about recording what it still owes, and all of that honesty is only worth having if somebody is watching. Nothing in the repository arranged for anybody to be. Now the numbers leave the database on every scrape, and production refuses to start without somewhere to send an exception.

What still does not exist. Infrastructure. The pipeline needs five secrets pointing at a real target; the backup script needs a schedule and an offsite bucket; the metrics need a scraper and alert thresholds; the WAL archiving needs a production instance to be configured on. Those are procurement and provisioning tasks, and they are the reason a real deployment is still some distance away even with the code in place.

Final certification

Fail

Production certification: FAIL — not production ready

One blocker remains, and it is disqualifying on its own: not one of the five external integrations has ever exchanged a packet with the provider it will depend on. Five of the original six are closed — the demonstration administrator, the localhost public URLs, deployment, backup and recovery, and observability — and each was re-verified rather than assumed.

I want to be precise about what this verdict does and does not say, because the two halves of it point in opposite directions.

It does not say the software is untrustworthy with money. On the evidence I gathered, the opposite is true to an unusual degree. I tried to forge receipts through the API and through raw SQL, to make an agent assert a payment, to replay webhooks, to race concurrent confirmations, to rewrite a commission, to alter a paid transaction by changing its rate, to read another agent's records, to reach government endpoints as an agent, to forge a token, to inject SQL, and to delete the audit trail. Every one of those failed, most of them at the database rather than in application code, which is the difference between a control and a convention. The commission engine is exact to the kobo and refuses to pay until government has actually been paid. Offline mode cannot express a payment at all. That is a well-built revenue platform.

Revision 10 qualifies that in one specific way, and it is worth being exact about how. The seventeen defects it found were not a different platform from the one described above; they were the same rules, correctly stated, enforced one step short. Migration 040 raised the standard from “the gateway said so” to “the State has the money”, and four separate places went on asking whether a settlement was linked rather than whether it had received anything. That is what a raised bar looks like on the way up: the statement of the rule moves first and the enforcement catches up behind it, and the gap between the two is invisible until somebody attacks it. Somebody has now attacked it, which is why those four are closed and why the question is asked in the same words in all four places.

The uncomfortable finding is not any of the seventeen. It is that two of them were held in place by tests — an assertion that an officer’s private note about a taxpayer was returned to anonymous callers, and one that accepted a unique-index violation as proof of a settlement rule. Both passed on every run for as long as they existed. A test suite is evidence about a platform only to the extent that somebody has recently asked what each test would still catch, and this report has now twice found the answer to be “less than it appears”.

What it said, in revision 1, was that a well-built application is not a production system. Four of those six gaps have since been closed in code, and the closing was worth doing properly: the restoration test was performed rather than planned, and the thing it proves is not that the rows came back but that the restored database still refuses a forged receipt. Two of the fixes were small and prevented real day-one harm — receipts whose QR codes reached nothing, an invitation to create a government administrator with a published password.

The one that remains cannot be closed by writing anything. Five integrations have never been asked a real question, and no amount of care in the adapters substitutes for asking. The adapters fail closed, which is right, and a mapping that is wrong rather than unrecognised sails straight past that — quietly, for weeks, looking like somebody else's outage. There is now a harness that would catch it in an afternoon against a sandbox. Somebody has to run it.

The repository says the same thing about itself in README.md §Status and docs/SECURITY.md §Known gaps. That honesty is worth noting in a certification report, because the most common reason a platform like this fails in production is that nobody wrote down what was still missing. Here it was written down, and this audit largely confirms it — with sixty-three defects added that had not been recorded, seventeen of them at this revision.

Re-certification should be straightforward once the remaining items in §11 are complete. Nothing I found requires rearchitecting anything — including the seventeen defects of revision 10, every one of which was a rule stated correctly somewhere and enforced one step short of where it needed to be.
